# The Odyssey -- Movie Review | Yaron Brook Show

## Topics

- Christopher Nolan's "The Odyssey" (visual craft, storytelling, casting) — about half the show — He recommends seeing it, ideally on the biggest IMAX screen, calling it visually spectacular and well-told despite complex flashback structure, and dismisses the right-wing "woke casting" controversy (Helen, the trans actor, Elliot Page) as irrelevant and stupid since none of it affects the story.
- Nolan's philosophical/thematic changes vs. Homer's Odysseus — the largest chunk of the show — He argues Nolan replaces Homer's celebration of reason and cunning (Odysseus as "polytropos," the man of many-formed intelligence) with a Christianized story of sin, guilt, and redemption, which he finds a weaker, less interesting theme that strips the hero of his defining intelligence and makes the movie emotionally cold.
- The gods' ambiguous role in the movie — moderate time — He criticizes this as an aesthetic flaw: Nolan keeps the gods (Athena, Poseidon, Zeus) steering events and predicting outcomes but never clarifies whether they're real, leaving Odysseus's fate to feel arbitrary rather than earned by his own agency or intelligence.
- "Zeus's law" and its use as a stand-in modern golden rule — moderate — He argues Nolan stretches Zeus's law (originally about hospitality toward possible disguised gods, useful in a trading society) into an anachronistic golden-rule/anti-violence message that doesn't really fit Bronze Age warfare or the sack of Troy.
- Comparisons to other adaptations (Ray Harryhausen films, Tennyson's "Ulysses," Denis Villeneuve's films, the Atlas Shrugged movies) — brief, in Q&A — He judges Harryhausen's films poorly made despite showing active gods, praises Tennyson's poem's craft without recalling details, likes Villeneuve's Dune and Sicario, and contrasts this movie favorably against the "truly horrific" Atlas Shrugged adaptations since Rand's theme is inseparable from her plot in a way Homer's isn't.
- Ayn Rand/Objectivism and economics (Q&A tangent) — brief — He restates that Objectivism has no economic theory of its own; it just applies Objectivist ethics/politics (selfishness as virtue, individual flourishing) to judge what a free economy would look like, distinguishing this from scarcity-based mainstream economics.
- Vaccines argument with a viewer (Q&A) — brief — He reasserts, as in past exchanges, that the anti-vax viewer is cherry-picking biased sources against the overwhelming consensus of pediatricians and scientists, and that ignoring mainstream/expert consensus wholesale is "perverse."

## Running threads

- His recurring line that Nolan's heroes (Oppenheimer, now Odysseus) are always flawed, guilt-ridden figures who potentially bring about world destruction — he ties this movie explicitly back to his prior take on Oppenheimer's ambiguity and names Interstellar as the one exception with a guilt-free hero.
- His ongoing dispute with a viewer (Jacob) over vaccines — he again cites the near-unanimous pediatrician/scientist consensus and accuses the viewer of faith-based cherry-picking, continuing a fight referenced as long-running ("every vax fact you present...").
- The Bronze Age collapse / Greek Dark Ages as historical backdrop he keeps returning to across the show — he uses it to argue civilizational collapse comes from internal rot, not external shock, tying it loosely to Nolan's "Zeus's law" collapse theme.

## Moves

- He refuses to render aesthetic verdicts ("good movie" vs "bad movie") without claiming expert-level command of film craft, but readily separates that from judging a work's philosophical/thematic content.
- He judges an adaptation on its own terms as a standalone artwork, not by fidelity to the source material, invoking Rand's idea that art expresses an artist's selective metaphysical value judgments.
- He repeatedly measures a story's value by whether it centers reason/intelligence as the source of a hero's efficacy, and marks its absence as the key flaw.
- He appeals to historical/period context (Bronze Age morality, absence of philosophy) to argue against anachronistically imposing modern moral categories like "war crimes" onto ancient stories.
- In disputes (vaccines), he leans on expert/majority consensus as the standard, treating rejection of it as evidence of biased, faith-based reasoning.
